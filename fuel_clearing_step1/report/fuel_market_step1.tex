\begin{abstract}
\noindent AeroMAPS is currently \emph{told} the share of each fuel and computes the
resulting cost. This work inverts that: a convex optimisation programme is given a
policy --- an obligation, a penalty, growth limits, capacities --- and returns both the
volume of each fuel produced and the price at which it clears. The programme is solved
once over all regions, all pathways and all prospective years, so it anticipates: it
builds ahead of an obligation it can see coming.

This report states the model in full, describes every parameter, and separates two
classes of result that are easy to confuse: those obtained from the optimisation kernel
\emph{alone}, at a demand fixed from outside, and those obtained with the kernel
\emph{chained into AeroMAPS}, where the price it sets feeds airline costs, airfares and
therefore back into demand. It reports what was verified, what was found to be broken
along the way, and what the mode cannot yet do.

The headline results: with scarcity switched off the new mode reproduces the existing
one to $1.9\times10^{-9}$ across the whole chain; with scarcity on it produces a
quantity AeroMAPS has never had --- a scarcity rent.

That rent turned out to be exactly what made the coupled fixed point hard to reach, and
chasing it down is the other half of this report. The market's price is a Lagrange
multiplier; a multiplier is a subgradient of a piecewise-smooth value function; and where
that function kinks the price is an \emph{interval} rather than a number, which no
fixed-point iteration can be asked to converge on. Giving the energy balance a demand
slope --- quantity from supply, price from demand, as in any capacity-constrained market
--- makes the price single-valued again, and is constructed so that it is exactly inert
once the loop settles. The coupled model now converges at full marginal pricing, in 60
sweeps and with no acceleration, to a solution of the unmodified programme.

A five-pathway bench then shows that how a supply limit is shaped matters only where a
limited fuel is the marginal one, and that where every fuel is at its limit the price comes
from demand. The report closes with the decisions framing step~2 --- one optimisation,
perfect foresight, regions interacting through one global pool --- and its first two steps:
the pool's volume side in the kernel, and flows between regions carried through AeroMAPS
with an explicit matrix standing in for the market.
\end{abstract}

\section{What this is, and what it answers}

The existing AeroMAPS energy chain answers: \emph{given these fuel shares, what does the
scenario cost?} It cannot answer \emph{given this policy, what fuel gets made?}, because
the quantity produced in response to a policy is exactly what a market determines and
the current model is handed it as an input.

The fuel market adds a second mode, opt-in and default off. Both modes coexist; they
answer different questions. The new one takes an obligation, a penalty for missing it,
a production cost and a set of physical limits per pathway, and returns:

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{volumes} --- how much of each fuel is produced, in each region, each year;
  \item \textbf{prices} --- what the energy is worth at the margin, what the obligation
        costs to comply with, and what a growth limit is worth relaxing.
\end{itemize}

The prices are not assumed or calibrated. They are the Lagrange multipliers of the
programme's constraints, which is what makes convexity worth paying for: a convex
programme has one optimum, and its multipliers are certified prices that do not depend
on where the solver started.

\section{The model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The programme}

Over regions $r \in \{1..R\}$, pathways $p \in \{1..P\}$ and prospective years
$t \in \{1..T\}$, with decision variables $q_{r,p,t} \ge 0$ (energy produced, MJ) and
$x_{r,t} \ge 0$ (obligation released by paying the penalty, MJ):

\begin{equation}
\min_{q,\;x}\;\; \sum_{t} \delta_t \left[
  \sum_{r,p} \underbrace{\left( c_{r,p,t}\, q_{r,p,t}
    + \frac{c_{r,p,t}\,\gamma_{r,p}\,K_{r,p,t}}{n+1}
      \left(\frac{q_{r,p,t}}{K_{r,p,t}}\right)^{n+1} \right)}_{\text{production cost, integral of the marginal cost}}
  \;+\; \sum_{r} B_{r,t}\, x_{r,t}
\right]
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}

subject to, for every region $r$ and year $t$:

\begin{align}
\sum_{p} q_{r,p,t} &= D_{r,t}
  && \text{energy balance} && \to \lambda^{E}_{r,t} \label{eq:balance}\\[1mm]
\sum_{p \in \mathcal{S}_r} q_{r,p,t} + x_{r,t} &\ge m_{r,t}\, D_{r,t}
  && \text{obligation} && \to \lambda^{M}_{r,t} \label{eq:mandate}\\[1mm]
\sum_{p \in \mathcal{N}_r} q_{r,p,t} + x^{\mathrm{sub}}_{r,t} &\ge \mu_{r,t}\, D_{r,t}
  && \text{sub-obligation} && \to \lambda^{S}_{r,t} \label{eq:submandate}\\[1mm]
q_{r,p,t} &\le s_{r,p} + (1+g_{r,p})\, q_{r,p,t-1}
  && \text{growth limit} && \to \lambda^{R}_{r,p,t} \label{eq:rampup}
\end{align}

with $q_{r,p,0} \equiv q^{\mathrm{init}}_{r,p}$ given. $\mathcal{S}_r$ is the set of
pathways \emph{eligible} for region $r$'s obligation and $\mathcal{N}_r \subseteq
\mathcal{S}_r$ the narrower set for its sub-obligation.

Three properties of this programme carry the design.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{One solve, not one per year.} Constraint~\eqref{eq:rampup} ties $t$ to $t-1$, so
solving year by year would discard anticipation. Sustainable output above the obligation
in early years is a \emph{result}, not an artefact: with perfect foresight the cheapest
way to meet a step is to be ready before it arrives.

\textbf{Convex, so the multipliers are prices.} $\lambda^{E}$ \emph{is} the marginal cost
of energy; $\lambda^{M}$ \emph{is} the compliance price. A fixed point found by
iteration would not come with that guarantee.

\textbf{The growth limit is a sum, not a maximum} (\S\ref{sec:convexity}).
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Lagrange multipliers, and the exact sense in which they are prices}
\label{sec:duality}

The claim in the box above --- ``$\lambda^{E}$ \emph{is} the marginal cost of energy'' ---
is the load-bearing one in this whole design, and it is worth deriving rather than
asserting, because the same derivation shows precisely where it stops holding.

\paragraph{The Lagrangian.} Write the objective~\eqref{eq:objective} as $C(q,x)$ and
attach a multiplier to each constraint, signed so that every multiplier is
non-negative on an inequality:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}(q,x;\lambda) = C(q,x)
&+ \sum_{r,t} \lambda^{E}_{r,t}\Big(D_{r,t} - \textstyle\sum_{p} q_{r,p,t}\Big)\\
&+ \sum_{r,t} \lambda^{M}_{r,t}\Big(m_{r,t} D_{r,t} - \textstyle\sum_{p \in \mathcal{S}_r} q_{r,p,t} - x_{r,t}\Big)\\
&+ \sum_{r,p,t} \lambda^{R}_{r,p,t}\Big(q_{r,p,t} - s_{r,p} - (1+g_{r,p})q_{r,p,t-1}\Big).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Stationarity is a statement about margins.} Setting
$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial q_{r,p,t} = 0$ for a pathway produced in strictly positive
quantity gives, after dividing through by the discount factor $\delta_t$:
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{c_{r,p,t}\Big(1 + \gamma_{r,p}\big(\tfrac{q_{r,p,t}}{K_{r,p,t}}\big)^{n}\Big)}_{\text{marginal production cost}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\lambda^{R}_{r,p,t} - (1+g_{r,p})\,\tfrac{\delta_{t+1}}{\delta_t}\lambda^{R}_{r,p,t+1}}_{\text{scarcity of the right to grow}}
\;=\;
\lambda^{E}_{r,t} + \lambda^{M}_{r,t}\,\mathbf{1}[p \in \mathcal{S}_r].
\label{eq:stationarity}
\end{equation}
Read from the right: what a unit of this fuel is \emph{worth} to the system --- the
energy it supplies, plus the obligation it discharges if it is eligible --- equals what
it costs at the margin, plus the shadow value of the growth room it consumes. That is an
identity, not a modelling assumption. Three things follow from it directly.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=5mm,itemsep=1mm]
\item \textbf{Equation~\eqref{eq:stationarity} is why the ramp-up multiplier never
appears in any published price.} It is already inside $\lambda^{E}$ and $\lambda^{M}$. A
model that added $\lambda^{R}$ to the price the airline pays would count it twice.
\item \textbf{A pathway not produced} has $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial q \ge 0$ instead:
it is worth less than it costs. The programme's choice of which fuels to build is
exactly this comparison.
\item \textbf{The term $-(1+g)\,\lambda^{R}_{t+1}$ is anticipation, written down.} Growth
room used this year buys growth room next year. Its sign is negative: building now is
\emph{worth} something later. This single term is why the solve builds ahead of an
obligation (\S\ref{sec:kernelresults}), and it exists only because all years are solved at
once.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Complementary slackness.} For every inequality, $\lambda \ge 0$, the slack is
$\ge 0$, and their product is zero:
\begin{equation}
\lambda^{M}_{r,t}\cdot\Big(\textstyle\sum_{p \in \mathcal{S}_r} q_{r,p,t} + x_{r,t} - m_{r,t}D_{r,t}\Big) = 0 .
\label{eq:cs}
\end{equation}
An obligation that is over-met has \emph{no} compliance price; one that is exactly met
has one. This is a testable property rather than an interpretation, and it is what the
parametrised test over seven regimes checks (\S\ref{sec:verif}). Every degeneracy
found in this work was found by it failing.

\paragraph{The envelope theorem is the actual claim.} Let $V(D)$ be the optimal value of
the programme as a function of the demand vector. Then at an optimum
\begin{equation}
\lambda^{E}_{r,t} \;=\; \frac{\partial V}{\partial D_{r,t}} .
\label{eq:envelope}
\end{equation}
The multiplier is the derivative of the cost of the whole system with respect to asking
it for one more MJ --- every knock-on effect included, across pathways, across years,
through the ramp-up chain. \emph{That} is what makes it a price and not a bookkeeping
quantity, and it is unavailable from any allocation rule: a rule can report what each
fuel cost, but only an optimum can report what the next unit would cost.

\paragraph{Why convexity is not a technicality.} Equation~\eqref{eq:envelope} needs
strong duality, which for this programme follows from Slater's condition (the feasible
set has a relative interior whenever the obligation is reachable, and the buy-out $x$
guarantees that it is). Convexity also delivers what no iterative scheme can: the
optimum is global and the duals are certified, so the answer does not depend on where
the solver started. This is the entire reason the market is a dedicated convex solve
rather than another block inside the MDA (\S\ref{sec:mda}).

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The caveat that the rest of this report is about.} Equation~\eqref{eq:envelope}
is an equality only where $V$ is differentiable. In general the correct statement is
\[
\lambda \in \partial V(D) \qquad\text{(the \emph{sub}differential)},
\]
and $V$ is piecewise smooth, not smooth: it has a kink wherever the set of tight
constraints changes. At such a point $\partial V$ is an \emph{interval}, every member of
it satisfies the KKT conditions, and the solver returns one of them with no way to
prefer another. The price is genuinely not determined by the cost side there.

Nothing is wrong with the optimisation. But a quantity that is set-valued cannot be the
input to a fixed-point iteration, and that --- not stiffness, not scaling, not the
solver --- is why the coupled loop failed at $w=1$ (\S\ref{sec:convergence}), and what
\S\ref{sec:fix} does about it.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The prices the model produces}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{9.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Symbol} & \textbf{From} & \textbf{Reads as} \\
\midrule
$\lambda^{E}_{r,t}$ & \eqref{eq:balance} & What the next unit of energy costs. Set by
  whichever pathway is on the margin. \\
$\lambda^{M}_{r,t}$ & \eqref{eq:mandate} & What one more unit of obligation costs.
  Bounded above by $B$. \textbf{Zero in any year where the market is already ahead of
  the obligation} --- a slack constraint costs nothing. \\
$\lambda^{S}_{r,t}$ & \eqref{eq:submandate} & The same, for the narrower target. \\
$\lambda^{R}_{r,p,t}$ & \eqref{eq:rampup} & What it would be worth to be allowed to grow
  faster. Zero where the limit is not in the way. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent The \emph{marginal price} of a pathway aggregates the constraints one unit of
it relaxes:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MC}_{r,p,t} \;=\; \lambda^{E}_{r,t}
  \;+\; \lambda^{M}_{r,t}\,\mathbf{1}[p \in \mathcal{S}_r]
  \;+\; \lambda^{S}_{r,t}\,\mathbf{1}[p \in \mathcal{N}_r].
\label{eq:marginal}
\end{equation}
A sub-mandated unit carries both obligation multipliers. That is not double counting: it
genuinely relaxes two distinct constraints.

\subsection{What the airline actually pays}
\label{sec:whopays}

\emph{This section answers a question the earlier write-ups left implicit.}

Two prices exist for each pathway and they are not the same. The \textbf{average cost}
is what the fuel produced actually cost, per unit:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AC}_{r,p,t} = c_{r,p,t}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma_{r,p}}{n+1}
  \left(\frac{q_{r,p,t}}{K_{r,p,t}}\right)^{n}\right),
\end{equation}
against the marginal price \eqref{eq:marginal}. The gap between them is
\textbf{inframarginal rent} --- profit earned on every unit produced more cheaply than
the last one. The pricing weight interpolates:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\mathrm{mfsp}^{\mathrm{mkt}}_{r,p,t}
  = (1-w)\,\mathrm{AC}_{r,p,t} + w\,\mathrm{MC}_{r,p,t}
  \;-\;\underbrace{\left(\mathrm{net}_{r,p,t}-\mathrm{gross}_{r,p,t}\right)}_{\text{tax wedge, \S\ref{sec:netgross}}}\;}
\label{eq:marketmfsp}
\end{equation}
and AeroMAPS's existing machinery then weights that across pathways into the one number
the airline sees, \var{\{type\}\_mean\_mfsp} $= \sum_p \mathrm{share}_{p}\cdot
\mathrm{mfsp}^{\mathrm{mkt}}_{p}$, which drives operating cost, airfare and demand.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{So: fuel cost, or compliance, or both?} The honest answer is \emph{both, always}
--- but not by addition, and this is the easiest thing in the model to get wrong.

\medskip
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Region A, 2050} & \textbf{Airline pays} & $\lambda^{M}$ & \textbf{SAF share} \\
\midrule
No obligation at all & 0.01200 & 0 & 0\,\% \\
70\,\% obligation, $w=0$ & \textbf{0.01982} & 0.01117 & 70\,\% \\
70\,\% obligation, $w=1$ & 0.01982 & 0.01117 & 70\,\% \\
\;+ scarcity, $w=0$ & 0.02115 & 0.07917 & 70\,\% \\
\;+ scarcity, $w=1$ & \textbf{0.06742} & 0.07917 & 70\,\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

\textbf{The obligation is paid for at $w=0$}: the bill goes from $0.01200$ to
$0.01982$, \textbf{a 65\,\% increase}. It is paid through the \emph{mix} --- the
obligation forces 70\,\% of the fuel to be the expensive kind --- not through a separate
charge. The cost of compliance is \emph{embedded}, not itemised.

\textbf{$\lambda^{M}$ is not added on top of that, and must not be.} It is the
\emph{marginal} cost of the obligation --- what one more unit of it would cost. The
average cost increment is $0.01982-0.01200 = 0.00782$, which is exactly
$0.70 \times \lambda^{M}$: share times marginal cost. Adding $\lambda^{M}$ to the
delivered price would give $0.03099$, a 158\,\% increase, \textbf{charging the obligation
twice}.

\textbf{What $w$ changes is average versus marginal, not paid versus unpaid.} At $w=1$
every unit is priced at the \emph{last} unit's cost. With nothing scarce that is the same
number (rows 2 and 3 are identical --- marginal equals average when the supply curve is
flat). With scarcity it is not: $0.06742$ against $0.02115$, and the difference is
inframarginal rent.

\textbf{It is never ``compliance only''.} $\lambda^{M}$ is a premium on top of the energy
price, never a substitute for it.

\textbf{The carbon tax} arrives separately, as its own \var{DirectOperatingCosts} line
--- which is exactly why \eqref{eq:marketmfsp} subtracts the tax wedge back out
(\S\ref{sec:netgross}). Same double-counting logic, different instrument.
\end{keybox}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{``Why average across pathways at all? Should there not be \emph{one} market price
--- or one per obligation, one for bio and one for e-SAF?''}

\medskip
\textbf{At $w=1$ there already is, and the weighted average is an identity rather than a
modelling choice.} Under uniform pricing every eligible producer is paid the same, so
\eqref{eq:marginal} takes one value per \emph{eligibility class}, not one per pathway.
Measured on a five-pathway case with a 70\,\% obligation and a 25\,\% e-SAF sub-target:

\medskip
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & $w = 0$ & $w = 0.5$ & $w = 1$ \\
\midrule
distinct per-pathway prices & 5 & 5 & \textbf{3} \\
share-weighted average & 0.041184 & 0.042087 & 0.042990 \\
$\lambda^{E} + m\lambda^{M} + m_s\lambda^{S}$ & 0.042990 & 0.042990 & \textbf{0.042990} \\
gap & $-0.001806$ & $-0.000903$ & $\mathbf{8\times10^{-13}}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

Five pathways, \textbf{three} prices: $0.012$ for fossil, $0.0322$ for ``counts towards the
broad obligation'', $0.0996$ for ``counts towards both''. One per obligation, plus the
energy price --- exactly the structure a certificate market has. \var{hefa\_fog} is paid
$0.0322$ though it cost $0.02317$; the $0.00903$ difference is its rent.

\medskip
$\lambda^{K}$ and $\lambda^{R}$ are not in it, and must not be added: they are already
\emph{inside} the price, on the producer's side. For each fuel, price = cost +
$\lambda^{K}$ + $\lambda^{R}$, net of next year's growth-limit rent (\S\ref{sec:foresight}):
they say how the payment splits between cost and rent. Adding them would charge the rent
twice.

\medskip
So $\lambda^{E}$, $\lambda^{M}$ and $\lambda^{S}$ \emph{are} the market prices.
\var{\{p\}\_market\_mfsp} is those three numbers re-expressed per pathway so that
AeroMAPS's existing per-pathway grammar can carry them, and the weighted average is the
arithmetic that reassembles them. Writing $\lambda^{E} + \sum_o m_o \lambda^{o}$ directly
would give the same number and would say more clearly what it is.

\medskip
\textbf{Below $w = 1$ the average stops being an identity and starts being an assumption},
and the gap in the table is exactly $w$ times the rent per unit --- at $w=0$,
$-0.001806 = 0.00903 \times 0.20$, \var{hefa\_fog}'s rent times its share. So $w$ is not
``average versus marginal'' in the abstract. It is \textbf{the share of scarcity rent the
airline is charged}, and the weighted average is how that dial is implemented.

\medskip
\textbf{One gap this exposes.} $\lambda^{S}$ is computed and never published: the
discipline emits \var{fuel\_market\_energy\_price} and
\var{fuel\_market\_compliance\_price} and no sub-mandate price, and it does not wire
\var{submandate\_share} through from the scenario at all --- the sub-mandate lives in the
kernel and is exercised only in \S\ref{sec:submandate}'s kernel-level case. A price vector
that is genuinely ``one per obligation per region'' needs the discipline to carry an
arbitrary number of obligations and publish a dual for each. It carries one.
\end{keybox}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{One thing the airline does \emph{not} pay, and arguably should.} The buy-out
payment $B\,x$ appears in objective~\eqref{eq:objective} but in no price output. If the
market releases part of the obligation, that penalty is borne by nobody in the cost
chain. At $w=1$ the \emph{rate} is signalled correctly ($\lambda^{M}$ is capped at $B$),
but the \emph{payment} is not accounted anywhere. This is a known gap, not a modelling
choice.
\end{keybox}

A useful consistency check falls out: with no scarcity at all, $\lambda^{E} = c_{k}$
(kerosene) and $\lambda^{M} = c_{s} - c_{k}$, so a sustainable pathway's marginal price
is exactly $c_{s}$ --- which is also its average cost. The two endpoints of
\eqref{eq:marketmfsp} coincide and $w$ is inert. \textbf{$w$ only matters where something
is scarce}, from \emph{either} the capacity term or a binding growth limit.

\subsection{Two choices that keep the problem convex}
\label{sec:convexity}

\paragraph{Capacity squeezes, it does not wall.} Production is not capped. Instead the
marginal cost rises with utilisation, $c\,(1+\gamma(q/K)^{n})$, whose integral appears in
\eqref{eq:objective}. A hard cap would make the price jump discontinuously at $q=K$; since
that price feeds airfares, which feed demand, which feeds fuel demand, a jumping price
makes the coupled loop oscillate instead of settle. Figure~\ref{fig:sat} shows the shape
and the rent.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering

\paragraph{The growth limit has to be a sum.} AeroMAPS's optimisation mode limits growth
by Eq.~(12) of the published work --- the \emph{more permissive} of a relative rate and a
fixed volume addition:
\begin{equation}
E_t \;\le\; \max\{\, (1+\tau)\,E_{t-1},\;\; E_{t-1} + \Delta E \,\}.
\label{eq:eq12}
\end{equation}
The region under a maximum of two affine functions is not a convex set
(Figure~\ref{fig:ramp}, right), so \eqref{eq:eq12} cannot be handed to a convex solver.
This is not a tolerance problem; the feasible set is the wrong shape.
Constraint~\eqref{eq:rampup} replaces the maximum with a \emph{sum}, which is its convex
outer relaxation: it admits everything \eqref{eq:eq12} admits, plus a margin
\textbf{measured at 0.73\,\%, independent of $g$}.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{explain_rampup.png}
\caption{Why the published growth limit cannot go into the programme, and what replaces

\label{fig:ramp}
\end{figure}

\noindent The seed $s$ in \eqref{eq:rampup} is Eq.~\eqref{eq:eq12}'s volume branch
$\Delta E$, so it is \emph{not} a free parameter: the published calibration
($0.2$\,EJ/yr at an ASK share of $0.1549$) puts it at about \textbf{1.5\,\% of annual
demand}. It matters --- on the bench the seed moves the compliance price by a factor
$4.3$ across a plausible range.

\subsection{What the discipline replaces}

In market mode the existing allocator \var{EnergyUseChoice} is \textbf{not instantiated}:
the market decides the volumes rather than being handed shares. It emits the same nine
output families under the same names, computed by a \emph{shared} function extracted from
the allocator rather than a second copy --- two copies would drift, and the failure is
silent, since a share family disagreeing with its volumes rescales CO$_2$ with no error
raised.

The mode is one configuration key, default off, propagated to each region as a
process-level keyword. It is refused outside the unified-MDA execution mode, and three
guards raise at set-up rather than producing plausible-looking numbers: drop-in fuels
only, top-down cost model only, exactly one residual pathway.

\subsection{The MDA, and why it is an awkward instrument for this particular coupling}
\label{sec:mda}

AeroMAPS closes its loops with a Multi-Disciplinary Analysis: GEMSEO orders the
disciplines, identifies the strongly coupled block (here 63 variables across two
regions), and sweeps it with Gauss--Seidel until a residual norm falls below tolerance.
For the couplings AeroMAPS was built around --- fleet renewal, energy intensity, traffic
--- this is exactly right. The market is a worse fit, and it is worth being explicit
about why, because the limitation is structural rather than a matter of settings.

\paragraph{An MDA is a fixed-point iteration, and it asks for more than it looks like.}
Gauss--Seidel converges when the composite map from coupling variables to coupling
variables is a contraction near the solution. In this loop that map is
\[
p \;\longmapsto\; D(p) \;\longmapsto\; \Lambda\big(D(p)\big) \;=\; p' ,
\]
where $D(\cdot)$ is the whole fleet--cost--airfare--elasticity chain and $\Lambda$ is the
market's price. Convergence therefore requires $\Lambda$ to be not merely bounded but
\emph{Lipschitz} in demand, with a small enough constant. At $w=0$ it is: the price is
the average cost, an explicit smooth function of the volumes. At $w=1$ it is a
multiplier, and \S\ref{sec:duality} established that a multiplier is a subgradient of a
piecewise-smooth value function. Its Lipschitz constant is infinite at every kink. No
choice of relaxation factor or acceleration method repairs that, because the failure is
in the map, not in the scheme applied to it --- a point this work initially got wrong
and had to retract (\S\ref{sec:defects}).

\paragraph{The disciplines exchange point values, and a set-valued quantity has none.}
The MDA's contract is that each discipline is a function: same inputs, same outputs. At a
kink the market is a \emph{correspondence} --- the KKT system admits an interval of
prices, and which one comes back is a property of the solver's trajectory. It is
consistent with the maths and inconsistent with the contract. Nothing in GEMSEO can
detect this; the discipline looks like it is returning a number, and it is.

\paragraph{The convergence test measures the wrong thing.} The residual is a norm over
all 63 coupling variables, in their own units. A market that has settled to eleven
digits can sit inside a residual dominated by \var{region\_B:ask} at $10^{12}$, and a
market that is flipping between two active sets can hide inside a residual that is
falling for unrelated reasons. Diagnosing \S\ref{sec:convergence} required instrumenting the
discipline itself --- and four successive instrumentation attempts were wrong before one
was right (\S\ref{sec:defects}), each producing a confident, false reading.

\paragraph{The market is solved many times to answer one question.} The convex programme
is solved \emph{in full} --- all regions, pathways and years, with perfect foresight ---
at every sweep. A converged coupled case costs 23 solves at $w=0$ and 60 at $w=1$; a
failing one costs 202. Each is a globally optimal answer to a question that is about to
be superseded.

\paragraph{What the alternative would be.} All of the above points the same way: the
demand response belongs \emph{inside} the programme rather than outside it. A single
optimisation maximising surplus net of cost, with demand as a decision variable, has no
outer loop, no set-valued exchange and no residual to interpret --- its optimality
conditions simply include the demand relation. That is a large change to how AeroMAPS is
organised and it is not proposed here.

What \emph{is} done here (\S\ref{sec:fix}) is the smallest useful step in that direction:
a local linearisation of the demand curve is passed into the programme, enough to make
the price single-valued, constructed so that it is exactly inert once the loop settles.
The MDA stays; it is simply no longer asked to iterate on a quantity that has no value
to iterate on.

\subsection{Decide on net, report on gross}
\label{sec:netgross}

The market chooses using \var{net\_mfsp} --- cost \emph{including} carbon tax and
subsidies --- because that is what determines which pathway is marginal. This is not
cosmetic: on the bench a carbon tax of only 5\,EUR/t already narrows the sustainable
premium by 3\,\%, and extrapolating the emission factors the premium reaches zero near
\textbf{164\,EUR/t}, where the ordering reverses and the market buys sustainable fuel
unprompted. A market clearing on gross cost could never produce that.

But \var{DirectOperatingCosts} adds the carbon tax as its own line. Publishing a
net-basis price would count the tax twice --- hence the wedge subtracted in
\eqref{eq:marketmfsp}. \textbf{Decide on net, report on gross, and the tax is counted
once.}

A \emph{passenger}-level tax is deliberately excluded from the objective: it is uniform
across fuels, so it cannot change which pathway is marginal. It still reaches the market
through demand, which is the correct channel.

\section{Verification}
\label{sec:verif}

\paragraph{An analytic case pins the conventions.} One region, one year, saturation known
in closed form, so both prices are known exactly. This caught a sign error that
documentation could not have: cvxpy reports the equality's multiplier negated, so the
energy price came out as $-0.012$ against an expected $+0.012$ --- a plausible number with
the wrong sign.

\paragraph{98 tests on the fuel market} --- 76 on the kernel, 8 on the discipline, 14 on
the flow plumbing of \S\ref{sec:trade} --- covering the balance, the obligation, the growth
limit, the capacity ceiling, the pool, scale invariance, continuity and input hygiene. The
full suite passes.

\paragraph{Complementary slackness, across seven regimes.} A multiplier may be non-zero
only where its constraint is tight. This single property is what all three real defects
(\S\ref{sec:defects}) violated, and nothing tested it. It is now parametrised over the
\emph{edges} --- zero obligation, full obligation, a constraint that cannot bind --- because
a mid-range case sees none of them. Worst residual: $1.4\times10^{-8}$, solver noise; the
phantom compliance price scored $0.46$ on the same measure.

\subsection{It reproduces the current allocator}

\subsection{The build-ahead: right motive, wrong instrument}
\label{sec:foresight}

At $g = 15\,\%$/yr against the ReFuelEU steps, region A holds \textbf{more} sustainable
fuel than it owes in every year between steps: 14.1\,\% in 2034 against a 6\,\%
obligation, 28.9\,\% in 2039 against 20\,\%. The growth limit cannot take it from 6\,\% to
20\,\% in the year the step lands, so it starts early, and it overshoots by the
\emph{minimum} that clears the next step, discounting penalising early spend.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The motive is real, and it is penalty avoidance.} The obligation
\eqref{eq:mandate} is written per year and there is no banking, so a sustainable MJ
produced in 2045 has no value \emph{as fuel} in 2046. But the growth limit ties what can be
produced next year to what is produced this year. Producing early therefore has a value:
it relaxes every later growth limit, and that relaxation is worth the discounted
compliance cost --- up to the penalty --- it saves when the step lands. The price
identity shows it directly: for a producing pathway,
\[
  \mathrm{MC}_{p,t} \;=\; c_{p,t} + \lambda^{K}_{p,t} + \lambda^{R}_{p,t}
  \;-\; \frac{(1+g)\,\lambda^{R}_{p,t+1}}{1+\rho},
\]
and the last term is exactly what producing now is worth to next year's allowance --- the
anticipation, priced. A buyer with perfect foresight who knows a step is coming
\emph{should} pay to be able to meet it.

\medskip
\textbf{Measured.} Sustainable share against the obligation, five-pathway kernel case,
hard capacities, varying only the growth limit:

With a slack growth limit the build-ahead is exactly zero: without a limit there is no
future penalty to anticipate. With one, the over-compliance sits just before a step
(2049, before 70\,\%), where it buys the most relief, and the same rows
\emph{under}-comply in 2039--41, where no anticipation could have ramped fast enough and
the penalty is simply paid.

\medskip
\textbf{What is wrong is the instrument.} Because the growth limit is written on
\emph{production}, the only way the programme can say ``be able to meet 2050'' is to make
the fuel in 2049 and burn it. In reality the same anticipation takes the form of plants
built ahead of the step, or of airlines signing offtake contracts early so that the plants
reach financing --- neither of which burns 8 to 14 extra points of the mix a year early.
The \emph{volume} of early sustainable fuel is therefore overstated, with the early fuel
bill and early CO$_2$ savings; the \emph{existence} of anticipation is not. The fix is a
capacity variable (\S\ref{sec:next}).

\medskip
\emph{Corrected.} Earlier versions of this section first called the build-ahead ``what a
firm reading a published mandate would do'', then withdrew that on the grounds that
nothing in the programme rewards buying early. The second statement was too strong: the
growth limit is exactly such a reward.
\end{keybox}

\noindent \textbf{Perfect foresight is kept} (\S\ref{sec:next}). The case against it --- one
planner's programme standing in for separate agents --- reduces to three assumptions,
stated rather than changed: there is no banking of compliance; one discount rate prices the
airline's fuel bill and the producer's plant alike (the capacity variable addresses this by
annuitising capex at a producer financing rate); every announced obligation is taken as
fully credible.

The penalty is needed exactly once, at the 2035 step and only at the tightest growth
limit. The compliance price is \textbf{zero in every year where the market is ahead}.

\subsection{The coupled fixed point, and why it failed at $w=1$}
\label{sec:convergence}

At $w=1$ the loop does not converge under AeroMAPS's default settings: the residual
stalls at $8.6\times10^{-2}$ after 200 sweeps. Damping helps but does not close it, and
acceleration --- which an earlier version of this report credited with fixing it --- does
not (\S\ref{sec:defects}).

The mechanism was first \emph{inferred} from the shape of the residual. It has since been
\emph{measured}. The kernel now reports an \var{active\_signature}: a digest of which
constraints are tight, taken from the primal slacks and never from the multipliers, since
whether a multiplier is non-zero is precisely the question that degenerates. Two sweeps
with the same signature solved the same linear system for their prices. The measurement
(\var{fuel\_clearing\_step1/convergence.py}, Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}) is
Table~\ref{tab:activeset}.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The mechanism, precisely.} In two (region, year) cells the binding constraint
trades places between \emph{the obligation binds} and \emph{the growth limit binds}. On
one side the obligation is met by volume and $\lambda^{M}$ is the cost of substituting
one fuel for another, about $0.012$\,\EURMJ. On the other the obligation has become
physically unreachable and the only way to discharge it is to pay --- so $\lambda^{M}$
snaps to the buy-out, $0.30$\,\EURMJ. The delivered price jumps 23--29\,\% when it does.

\textbf{The volume is identical on both sides.} It is pinned by the growth limit. Only
the price differs. That is not a solver artefact: at the crossing point the value
function has a genuine kink, relaxing the growth limit saves nothing (the obligation is
already met) while tightening it costs the buy-out, so the right and left derivatives
differ and $\partial V$ is the whole interval $[0.012,\,0.30]$ --- a factor of 25. This
is exactly the caveat of \S\ref{sec:duality}, met in practice.

\textbf{So $w$ decides whether the model couples to a well-defined function of its own
state}, not how volatile the price is. At $w=0$ the delivered price is the average cost:
a function of volumes, which move by 1--2\,\%. At $w=1$ it contains $\lambda^{M}$, and
at a kink $\lambda^{M}$ is not a function of the state at all.
\end{keybox}

\noindent Two explanations were tested and \textbf{rejected}. The bad years sit next to a
ReFuelEU step, so the staircase obligation looked responsible --- but replacing it with a
smooth ramp through the same points still fails: smoothing moves the boundary rather than
removing it. And the measured loop gain, $\approx 0.32$, is far below 1; a gain that low
cannot diverge, which is what ruled out ``the prices are volatile'' at the outset.

\subsection{The fix: quantity from supply, price from demand}
\label{sec:fix}

The diagnosis dictates the remedy. Where the supply curve is vertical --- a growth limit
exhausted, an obligation reachable only by buying out --- the quantity is determined and
the price is not. That is not a pathology; it is the ordinary situation in any
capacity-constrained market, and every such market resolves it the same way: \textbf{the
quantity comes from supply and the price comes from demand}. The programme was solving a
supply curve at a fixed quantity and then being asked for a price the supply side does
not contain.

So the balance is given a slope. Around the incoming demand $D_{r,t}$ and the price
$p^{0}_{r,t}$ that demand was formed at, introduce $a_{r,t}$ (free) and replace
\eqref{eq:balance} and \eqref{eq:mandate} with
\begin{equation}
\sum_{p} q_{r,p,t} = D_{r,t} + a_{r,t},
\qquad
\sum_{p \in \mathcal{S}_r} q_{r,p,t} + x_{r,t} \ge m_{r,t}\,\big(D_{r,t} + a_{r,t}\big),
\label{eq:elastic}
\end{equation}
adding to the objective the consumer surplus given up, to second order:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{r,t}\delta_t\left[-\,p^{0}_{r,t}\,a_{r,t} + \frac{a_{r,t}^{2}}{2\beta_{r,t}}\right],
\qquad \beta_{r,t} = \eta\,\frac{D_{r,t}}{p^{0}_{r,t}} \;\ge\; 0 .
\label{eq:surplus}
\end{equation}
The quadratic keeps the programme convex. Stationarity in $a_{r,t}$ then reads
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\lambda^{E}_{r,t} + m_{r,t}\,\lambda^{M}_{r,t} \;=\; p^{0}_{r,t} - \frac{a_{r,t}}{\beta_{r,t}}\;}
\label{eq:inversedemand}
\end{equation}
--- the delivered marginal price equals the inverse demand at the quantity served. The
left-hand side is what the airline faces per MJ: the energy, plus the share $m$ of
obligation that MJ drags with it (\S\ref{sec:whopays}). The identity holds to
$5\times10^{-11}$ in the kernel tests, and it is the entire mechanism. On a vertical
stretch of supply, $a$ moves until the price sits where demand wants it; the gap that
opens between that price and the marginal production cost is a \textbf{scarcity rent},
which is what a capacity-constrained producer earns, not an error.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{This is exact, not a relaxation.} At a fixed point of the coupling loop the price
the market returns \emph{is} the price the demand was formed at, so $p^{0} = \lambda^{E}
+ m\lambda^{M}$, hence $a = 0$ by \eqref{eq:inversedemand}, hence
\eqref{eq:elastic}--\eqref{eq:surplus} reduce to \eqref{eq:balance}--\eqref{eq:mandate}
identically. The loop therefore converges to a solution of the \emph{unregularised}
programme or it does not converge at all. There is no fixed point at which the added term
is still bending the answer. Measured: $|a|/D = 6\times10^{-11}$ at convergence.

\textbf{And $\eta$ does not have to be right.} It sets how far the price moves per sweep
and nothing else. Linearising the loop gives the requirement $\beta > \tfrac12|dD/dp|$:
over-stating the demand response is safe and merely slow, under-stating it by more than a
factor of two overshoots.
\end{keybox}

Two further consequences. First, a \textbf{negative result worth keeping}: anchoring the
\emph{volumes} instead --- a proximal term $\tfrac{\rho}{2}\|q - \hat q\|^2$ --- does not
work, and cannot, because the primal is identical on both sides of the kink. It moves
$\lambda^{M}$ across 0.6\,\% of the interval it is free in. It is retained as an optional,
separately weighted device for degeneracies in the \emph{primal}, off by default.

Second, all of this runs on \textbf{plain Gauss--Seidel with no acceleration}. The
acceleration that was previously thought to help is gone from the coupled grid along with
the claim.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{``Away from a kink the supply curve \emph{does} have a price at the quantity handed
in. Is it not bizarre to take the price from demand there --- and might the answer not be
the true crossing of supply and demand?''}

\medskip
The objection is right and the title of this section is what invites it. Away from a kink
the price is \emph{not} taken from demand, in the following precise sense.

\medskip
\textbf{There are two loops, and $\beta$ lives in the wrong one to matter.} The market
solves, given $(D, p^{0})$, for $q$ and $a$. The traffic loop then maps the price the
market returns into a new $D$. At a fixed point of the \emph{outer} loop the price the
market returns \emph{is} the price the demand was formed at, $p = p^{0}$; put that into
\eqref{eq:inversedemand} and $a/\beta = 0$, so $a = 0$. \textbf{The converged solve is a
solve of the rigid programme at the converged demand.} Where supply is single-valued the
reported price is the supply curve's own value, exactly as it should be.

\medskip
\textbf{Off the fixed point it does a great deal, and that is its job.} On a flat supply
segment at cost $c^{*}$ the solve picks $a = \beta(p^{0} - c^{*})$: the market anticipates
that the traffic model is about to move demand, because the price it is about to quote
differs from the one its demand was formed at, and it moves the quantity by its own
estimate of that response before quoting. That is a \textbf{Newton step on the outer
fixed-point equation}. $\beta$ is a \emph{preconditioner}, not an elasticity, and
$e = e_{0}(\beta-d)/(s+\beta)$ is the statement of exactly that.

\medskip
\textbf{Measured}, on the five-pathway \var{hard} case of \S\ref{sec:five}, whose clearing
sits on a flat segment in both regions --- precisely the case the objection is about:

A factor of four in $\beta$ moves the answer by $3\times10^{-9}$ --- the MDA tolerance,

\medskip
\textbf{So the honest description is narrower than the title.} ``Quantity from supply,
price from demand'' is what happens \textbf{on a vertical segment}, where it is the
mechanism --- and \S\ref{sec:five} measures a case in which it charges $0.238$\,\EURMJ\
for a fuel whose dearest producer charges $0.0997$. Everywhere else the same machinery
runs as an accelerator and the price still comes from supply.

\medskip
\textbf{It is not free.} Off the fixed point the market has cleared $D + a$ while
AeroMAPS's energy budget is $D$; \var{\_reconcile} takes the mix from the market and the
level from the budget, so an intermediate iterate carries a price computed at one quantity
and volumes scaled to another. They agree at convergence --- $|a|/D$ is $5.6\times10^{-8}$
at $\eta=0.5$ above --- which means \textbf{the MDA tolerance is doing real work}: stop the
loop early and the published price and the published volumes answer different questions.
\var{demand\_adjustment} is published so that this is checkable. No test asserts on it
today.
\end{keybox}

\textbf{Practical consequence:} $w=0$ was already solid. $w>0$ is now a scenario dial
rather than a research problem, at the cost of one parameter whose value affects the
route and not the destination.

\subsection{The last three cells: a tolerance below the solver's own precision}
\label{sec:tolerance}

After \S\ref{sec:fix} three cells still failed --- the whole stiffest-saturation column,
$n=16$, at $w=1$. The natural guess is that the demand slope is mis-set there, and the
linearised iteration supports it: writing the error propagation of the anchored step as
\begin{equation}
e \;=\; e_0\,\frac{\beta - d}{s + \beta},
\qquad d = \Big|\tfrac{dD}{dp}\Big|,\quad s = 1/S'(q),
\label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
a \emph{stiff} supply curve drives $s \to 0$ and removes all tolerance for under-stating
$\beta$. (Equation~\eqref{eq:contraction} also explains Table~\ref{tab:ablation}: at
$\beta = d$ it gives $e = 0$, one-step convergence, which is why $\eta=0.5$ beat
$\eta=1.0$ --- the anchored scheme is a modified Newton method whose slope is set by hand.)

\textbf{The guess was wrong, and the active-set signature said so at once.}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{A convergence tolerance is only meaningful against the precision of the
disciplines underneath it.} Coupling a conic solver into a fixed-point loop imports that
solver's noise floor into the loop's stopping rule. The symptom --- a run reporting
non-convergence having in fact converged --- is indistinguishable from a real failure
unless the discipline itself is instrumented.

This is the third instance of the report's recurring shape. \S\ref{sec:defects}: a
programme that solved and was wrong. \S\ref{sec:convergence}: a price that had no single
value. Here: a loop that had arrived and was told it had not.
\end{keybox}

\noindent \textbf{A finding about the code rather than the model.} That tolerance was
hard-coded at $10^{-10}$ in two places in \var{multi\_regional\_process.py} and could not
be set from a scenario; the \var{TODO} beside it said as much. Assigning it on the chain
after construction silently does nothing, which is how the first attempt at this
measurement produced a false negative. It is now \var{regionalisation.mda\_tolerance}
(with \var{mda\_max\_iter}), defaulting to what it was, so nothing that ran before runs
differently.

\section{Five pathways: a curve that bends against one that jumps}

\subsection{So: keep $\gamma$, $n$, $K$?}

\textbf{Keep $K$. Drop $\gamma$ and $n$ as the default.}

The argument is not that the bent curve is wrong. It is that at $n=4$ the market charges
$0.065$ for the marginal eligible unit --- a number that is not any pathway's cost, that no
plant would quote, and that is an interpolation between $0.0397$ (\var{atj}) and $0.0997$
(\var{electrofuel}). \textbf{It is a price for a fuel nobody makes.} The hard cap says
instead that the next unit after \var{atj} runs out is an \var{electrofuel} unit at
\var{electrofuel}'s cost --- a statement an engineer can check, and disagree with.

$\gamma$ and $n$ also cannot be calibrated: there is no measurement whose answer is
``$\gamma=1$, $n=4$''. $K$ can be. It is a plant fleet, in MJ/yr, and ENSPRESO and the
ReFuelEU impact assessment have numbers for it.

\textbf{And the reason for the soft form has expired.} It was chosen because a hard cap
whipsawed the loop. The hard-cap runs here converge in 91 Gauss--Seidel sweeps, against 44
for $n=4$ and 97 for $n=16$: the staircase is \emph{not} the slowest of the three.

Keep $\gamma$ and $n$ available. They are the right tool for a pathway that genuinely has
a rising cost curve rather than a wall --- a feedstock drawn from a graded resource, where
the next tonne really is dearer than the last. That is a \emph{different} physical claim
from ``the plant makes 2\,Mt/yr'', and the model should be able to say both.

and so the wrong picture of demand.

The clearing point of the hard run sits at the \emph{foot of the}
\var{electrofuel} \emph{step}, on a flat segment --- not on a riser. That bounds the whole
concern about step-function duals: \textbf{with an uncapped backstop in the set, the
staircase never leaves the price undefined.} Some pathway is always marginal at its own
cost. The vertical segment can only bite when every eligible route is limited at once.

So the \var{all\_capped} case builds exactly that: \var{electrofuel} capped too, at
1.0 EJ/yr, putting total eligible capacity at 8.6 EJ/yr. The traffic loop then walks
demand down until the obligation exhausts it --- and in one run, the two regions land in
the two different regimes:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{2050, $w=1$} & \textbf{eligible/cap} & $\lambda^{E}$ & $\lambda^{M}$ &
  \textbf{paid} & \textbf{dearest} & \textbf{rent above all} \\
\midrule
region A & 7.818/8.6 \textbf{spare} & 0.01244 & 0.08725 & 0.09970 & 0.09970 & $-0.00000$ \\
region B & 8.600/8.6 \textbf{full} & 0.01244 & \textbf{0.22542} &
  \textbf{0.23786} & 0.09970 & $\mathbf{+0.13817}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{What the multipliers do when the price really does come from demand.}

\medskip
Region A sits on a flat segment: the eligible unit is paid exactly \var{electrofuel}'s
cost to five decimals, and no multiplier exceeds a cost difference. $\lambda^{E}$ is the
residual's cost, $\lambda^{M}$ is the marginal eligible cost minus it, and every
inframarginal pathway's multiplier is the pure cost gap
$\lambda^{K}_{p} = (\lambda^{E}+\lambda^{M}) - c_{p}$.

\medskip
Region B sits on the vertical. Supply fixes the \emph{quantity} --- $mD$ equals the
capacity to the digit --- and says nothing about the price.
Equation~\eqref{eq:inversedemand} picks it, with $a \to 0$, so $\lambda^{E} + m\lambda^{M}
= p^{0}$; the split is still pinned from the supply side because kerosene is still
available, so
\[
  \boxed{\;\lambda^{M} = \frac{p^{0} - c_{\mathrm{kerosene}}}{m}\;}
\]
\textbf{The compliance price becomes a demand-side object}: willingness to pay, net of the
fossil cost, divided by the obligation share. Taking $p^{0}$ from the \emph{previous}
iterate --- an independent number, not a rearrangement --- this reproduces $\lambda^{M}$ to
$2.8\times10^{-13}$ in region A and $8.8\times10^{-13}$ in region B.

\medskip
The consequence is the $0.23786$ above: \textbf{2.4 times the most expensive thing anyone
makes}, with $0.138$\,\EURMJ\ of it above every cost in the model. That is not an error.
It is the scarcity rent, and it is what a price does when the last unit cannot be produced
at any cost. Traffic falls 46.9\,\%.

\medskip
\textbf{Two things to be careful of.} The $1/m$ factor \emph{leverages} the compliance
price: at $m=0.7$ a $0.01$ error in the delivered price is a $0.014$ error in
$\lambda^{M}$. And on a vertical segment $\beta$ stops being a preconditioner and becomes
the demand curve whose crossing sets the price --- so that $0.238$ should be read as
``what this demand curve implies'', not as a forecast, until the coupled response is
calibrated against the traffic model it stands in for.
\end{keybox}

\noindent Two costs. \var{all\_capped} needs $\eta = 2$, not the bench's $0.5$: on a
vertical segment the loop's true response is stiff, and at $\eta=0.5$ it does not converge
in 900 sweeps. \S\ref{sec:fix}'s rule --- over-state $\beta$ rather than under-state it ---
is here the difference between an answer and none. And at $w=0$ the case does not converge
at all (residual 0.09 after 900 sweeps), which is unexpected enough to record: $w=0$ prices
at average cost, so the loop should be \emph{less} reactive. That is unexplained, and so
are the two scaled-down probes above --- three failures, all where every eligible route is
at its limit.

\subsection{What this measurement does not establish}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{The capacity levels are illustrative.} 2.0/2.8/2.8 EJ/yr were sized to put
    the obligation inside the capped set, not taken from a resource assessment. Every
    number in table~\ref{tab:five} moves with them. The \emph{structural} result does not:
    it turns on which pathway is marginal, not on where the caps are.
  \item \textbf{\var{capacity\_limit} is one number per pathway, for all regions and
    years.} A real plant fleet grows; this one does not. Per-region and per-year ceilings
    are a grammar change, not a kernel change --- the kernel already takes an $(R,P,T)$
    array.
  \item \textbf{Region A and region B differ in traffic growth too} (3.0 against
    4.5\,\%/yr), so the contrast mixes two causes. The claim survives it --- an identical
    $\lambda^{M}$ across three configurations cannot be explained by growth --- but the
    \emph{sizes} are not like for like.
\end{itemize}

\section{Defects found, and one general lesson}
\label{sec:defects}

Three defects of the \emph{same family} were found, all of them producing
plausible-looking numbers rather than errors.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{A price for an obligation that does not exist.} Where $m=0$,
        constraint~\eqref{eq:mandate} is implied by $q,x \ge 0$ --- redundant, so its
        multiplier is free. The solver reported up to $0.0112$\,\EURMJ, roughly the whole
        cost of kerosene, in years with no obligation.
  \item \textbf{An indeterminate split at a 100\,\% obligation.} There the obligation
        forces conventional supply to zero, so \eqref{eq:balance} and \eqref{eq:mandate}
        have the same active rows and only their \emph{sum} is determined. The solver
        split the identical problem three different ways, including a \emph{negative}
        energy price.
  \item \textbf{A jump at a binding/slack boundary} --- \S\ref{sec:convergence}. The same
        thing dynamically, and the one that stopped the coupled solve.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent In every case a constraint stopped being independent and its multiplier became
arbitrary. The first two are pinned to their meaningful KKT values. The third could not
be pinned, because the discontinuity is real and neither branch is more meaningful than
the other --- it needed information the cost side does not contain, which is what
\S\ref{sec:fix} supplies.

\subsection{A global setting that reached the wrong pathway}
\label{sec:kerosene}

Found by an external review of this report, and present in \emph{every} coupled
measurement in it.

\var{FuelClearing.\_pathway\_setting} resolves a per-pathway override if one is given and
otherwise falls back to the global value --- for every pathway, including the residual.
All three coupled bench scripts pass \var{capacity} and \var{saturation\_intensity} at
the top level, meaning them for the pathway under study. So \textbf{kerosene was
saturating too}, at about 90\,\% utilisation, and its marginal cost rose with output.

The consequences were not confined to kerosene. Re-running the whole grid with the
residual exempted:

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=5mm]
  \item $w=0$ barely moves (at most $-1.6$ points, since the delivered price there is an
        average cost);
  \item $w=1$ moves \emph{up} by as much as \textbf{14 points} ($+99.6 \to +113.4$ at
        $\gamma=0.5$, $n=2$). Saturating kerosene raised $\lambda^{E}$ but lowered
        $\lambda^{M}$ by more, so it was suppressing the scarcity signal the measurement
        exists to show;
  \item measurement 4.5.2 lost two of its seven points and its ``rises then falls''
        finding with them.
\end{itemize}

The residual is now exempt from the global scarcity settings and takes the no-scarcity
default unless named explicitly under \var{pathways:} --- a fossil supply limit is a
legitimate thing to model, it simply has to be asked for. Six tests in
\var{test\_fuel\_clearing\_discipline.py}, which is also the first test of the discipline
rather than the kernel.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{And the check on the fix turned up a second thing.} With kerosene free,
$\lambda^{E}$ should equal the kerosene cost. It does not --- it sits $3.6\,\%$ above it,
by a gap that is \emph{identical in every year and both regions}: $0.00044350$.

That is exactly the carbon tax. $88.7\,\mathrm{gCO_2/MJ} \times 5\,\mathrm{EUR/tCO_2} =
0.00044350$. So the identity does hold, on the \emph{net} basis the programme decides on
(\S\ref{sec:netgross}) --- and \var{fuel\_market\_energy\_price} is published on that net
basis while \var{\{p\}\_market\_mfsp} is published gross, with the wedge stripped. Two
published prices, two bases, no note saying so. The duals are correct; the reporting is
inconsistent, and anyone differencing them would find a carbon tax they did not put
there. Recorded, not yet fixed: which basis a dual should be published on is a design
decision, not a bug fix.
\end{keybox}

\subsection*{Two claims this report previously made and now withdraws}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{``Acceleration fixes the \(w=1\) failure.''} It was true of the one cell
        it was measured on. Run over the grid, Secant acceleration returned 3 cells of
        12. It was a coincidence of trajectory, not a remedy: no sequence transformation
        can smooth a map that is genuinely discontinuous. The acceleration has been
        removed from the coupled grid.
  \item \textbf{``\(w>0\) is a research problem, not a scenario dial.''} It was, until
        the failure was measured rather than inferred. It is now a dial
        (\S\ref{sec:fix}).
\end{itemize}

\noindent Both errors share a shape with the defects above, and with the four successive
instrumentation attempts that each produced a confident and false reading of the coupled
loop: \emph{a measurement of one case, generalised}. The active-set signature exists so
that this particular question can be answered by looking rather than by arguing.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{A convex programme that \emph{solves} is not a programme that is \emph{right}.}
The saturation term was first written as $a\,q^{n+1}$ with $a = c\gamma/((n{+}1)K^{n})$
--- algebraically correct, numerically unusable: $a$ carries $K^{-n}$ and the coefficients
spanned 35 orders of magnitude at $n=16$. Half the parameter grid refused to solve; worse,
\emph{where it did solve it returned a suboptimal point}, 0.13\,\% dearer, which showed up
as a \textbf{3.4\,\% error in the delivered price} --- at the exact settings the whole
sensitivity table was built on. The solver reported \texttt{optimal} both times.

Rewriting it in the utilisation it is actually a function of,
$[c\gamma K/(n{+}1)]\,(q/K)^{n+1}$, made all 36 cells solve. The only thing that caught
the wrong answer was scoring the returned point against the objective \emph{independently
of the solver}.
\end{keybox}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{In the market, regions still do not interact.} Every constraint of
        \var{FuelClearing} is per region and the objective a plain sum, so solving jointly
        and separately agree to solver noise: no \textbf{leakage}, no competition for a
        feedstock. A global pool now exists in the kernel (\S\ref{sec:pool}) and flows are
        carried through AeroMAPS (\S\ref{sec:trade}), but the two are not yet joined.
  \item \textbf{One planner, with perfect foresight}, stands in for every agent: no banking
        of compliance, one discount rate for a fuel bill and a plant, every mandate fully
        credible (\S\ref{sec:foresight}). Kept deliberately (\S\ref{sec:next}).
  \item \textbf{$w>0$ needs a demand slope to converge} (\S\ref{sec:fix}). It does not
        affect the answer where supply is single-valued, but too small a value costs 200
        sweeps or the run. It should be derived rather than configured --- or made
        unnecessary by putting demand inside the kernel (\S\ref{sec:next}).
  \item \textbf{The MDA tolerance has to be chosen against the solver's precision}
        (\S\ref{sec:tolerance}). The market mode runs at $10^{-7}$; the default stays
        $10^{-10}$. Nothing warns a user who pairs them badly.
  \item \textbf{The elastic balance is a local linearisation}, exact at the fixed point and
        only there: enough to select a price, not enough to be a demand model.
  \item \textbf{The buy-out payment reaches no one} (\S\ref{sec:whopays}).
  \item \textbf{No learning curve} anywhere in AeroMAPS, so every price dynamic comes from
        the model's own scarcity terms.
  \item \textbf{Bottom-up costs are excluded ``for now''}: a vintage \emph{mix} makes average
        cost \emph{fall} with volume and the cost integral concave. Choosing vintages
        rather than reading a mean removes the obstacle (\S\ref{sec:next}).
  \item \textbf{2050 is the horizon} and also the steepest step, so results turning on
        ``what is built by the end'' are partly a terminal condition.
  \item \textbf{Emission factors, capacities and four of the five costs are assumed.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Market and traffic: put demand inside the kernel?}
\label{sec:demandinside}

\textbf{The problem.} The kernel is a supply-side market; traffic lives in AeroMAPS, and the
two meet through the Gauss--Seidel loop. That loop is where most of this report's difficulty
came from: price intervals at kinks (\S\ref{sec:fix}), a demand slope that must be over- rather
than under-stated, tolerances tied to the solver's precision (\S\ref{sec:tolerance}), and the
failures of \S\ref{sec:five}.

\textbf{What the traffic block actually is.} In \var{models\_operation\_cost\_top\_down\_feedback}
it is already a small market in equilibrium --- a linear airline supply curve
(\var{PassengerAircraftMarginalCost}) crossing an iso-elastic passenger demand
(\var{RPKElasticity}):
\begin{align*}
A &= a_t\,\mathrm{RPK} + b + \big(C^{\mathrm{nf}}_t - C_0\big) + e_t\,p + \tau_t
  && \text{airline supply}\\
\mathrm{RPK} &= \mathrm{RPK}^0_t\,\big(A/A_0\big)^{\varepsilon}
  && \text{passenger demand, } \varepsilon = -0.9\\
E &= e_t\,\mathrm{RPK} + E^{\mathrm{other}}_t
  && \text{fuel demand}
\end{align*}
with $a_t = 2(A_0 - C_0)/\mathrm{RPK}^0_t$, $b = 2C_0 - A_0$, $e_t$ in MJ per RPK and
$\mathrm{RPK}^0_t$ the traffic before any price effect.
Both integrate into terms a convex solver accepts. Passenger value
$U_t(\mathrm{RPK}) = A_0 (\mathrm{RPK}^0_t)^{-1/\varepsilon}\,
\mathrm{RPK}^{1+1/\varepsilon}/(1+1/\varepsilon)$ is concave (exponent $-0.111$ here, a power
cone); airline cost is quadratic. One programme can then hold fuel supply, airline supply
and passenger demand at once, maximising value minus all costs. Its optimality condition in
RPK \emph{is} AeroMAPS's airfare equation,
\[
  U_t'(\mathrm{RPK}) = a_t\,\mathrm{RPK} + b + C^{\mathrm{nf}}_t - C_0 + \tau_t
  + e_t\big(\lambda^{E} + m\,\lambda^{M}\big),
\]
with the fuel term equal to the single market price of \S\ref{sec:whopays}. The elastic
balance of \S\ref{sec:fix} is the first-order version of this: $\eta$ is the slope of a
tangent to a curve the kernel could hold exactly.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{7.4cm}p{7.8cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Moves when the fuel price moves $\rightarrow$ solved inside} &
\textbf{Does not move $\rightarrow$ an input, computed upstream} \\
\midrule
fuel price, fuel mix, airline costs and airfare, traffic, energy demand, fuel bill &
$\mathrm{RPK}^0$ (traffic before any price effect), non-fuel costs, MJ per RPK, taxes,
base-year calibration, each pathway's cost \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The left column is exactly the loop the MDA iterates on today. With demand inside,
one solve finds all of it together, so \textbf{that loop no longer needs an MDA}: AeroMAPS runs
its chain once afterwards, and a zero residual confirms the two agree. The right column does
not depend on the fuel price, so it arrives as ordinary inputs, with no dry run.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Why average-cost pricing does not fit this option.} The solve does not compute a price
and hand it to demand: it chooses fuel volumes and traffic \emph{together}. At the optimum the
last passenger-km is worth what it costs to serve, and that cost is a derivative --- the
marginal price $\lambda^{E} + m\lambda^{M}$. That is what an optimum is, not a rule added to it.
Average cost can still be imposed, as a constraint tying traffic to $\sum_p c_p q_p / \sum_p
q_p$; but that is a ratio of the solve's own variables inside a nonlinear equation, and the
problem stops being convex --- with it go the single certified answer and the multipliers as
prices. Average cost is better served by today's loop, which at $w=0$ converges easily
(16 solves, figure~\ref{fig:convergence}) because the average cost moves smoothly.

\medskip
It is also the less natural assumption: when supply is short, every seller gets the market
price, and paying average cost describes contracts or regulation. \textbf{So: $w=1$ with
demand inside, one solve; $w<1$ in today's loop mode}, with average cost and producer rents
reported as outputs either way.
\end{keybox}

\noindent \textbf{What else it costs.} Only some demand models fit: \var{RPKElasticity} yes;
\var{logistic\_income} probably (a multiplicative price index, unverified); the fleet-push
model no, since deliveries make energy demand depend on the traffic \emph{path} --- those keep
today's loop. The airline calibration is duplicated in the kernel, guarded by the one-sweep
test. It forces a pending AeroMAPS choice: whether the carbon tax sits inside or outside the
airline supply function. And whether freight energy responds to price is to be checked.
\textbf{Decided by D1--D2.}

\subsection{Supply: a capacity variable, using bottom-up data}
\label{sec:capacityvariable}

Soft saturation invents prices and cannot be calibrated (\S\ref{sec:five}); a fixed cap is a
plant fleet that never grows; and growth limits on production put anticipation on burning
fuel early (\S\ref{sec:foresight}). The option: decide \textbf{capacity additions by vintage},
production $\le$ installed capacity $\times$ load factor, growth limits on additions.

\textbf{How it gets its costs.} Each vintage --- the plants commissioned in a given year --- has
its own unit cost: that year's capex annualised, plus opex and resources. The kernel computes
these numbers itself before the solve, with the formula and inputs \var{BottomUpCost} uses
(capex, opex, lifespan, construction time, load factor, private discount rate), then decides
how much of each vintage to build. \var{BottomUpCost} is not run inside; fed the chosen
build-out afterwards through \var{\{p\}\_energy\_production\_commissioned}, which it already
reads, it reports the mean costs and should agree. Decision~6's objection does not arise,
because the programme chooses vintages rather than reading a mean. Pathways documented only
top-down need one extra number, the capital share of their cost.

It adds steps (one per vintage), not smoothness; what it adds is \emph{time} --- rents appear
while plants are short and pay for the next ones. Capex falling with the commissioning
\emph{year} fits; capex falling with \emph{cumulative} capacity (endogenous learning) is
non-convex and does not. \textbf{Decided by D3.}

\noindent Most of the gap is plumbing and linear constraints. The one structural step is the
discipline carrying \textbf{a list of obligations per region} and publishing one price per
obligation (\S\ref{sec:whopays}). Without shared supply, instruments in different regions still
cannot affect each other --- which is the pool's job.

\subsection{Regions through one global pool}

Each region \textbf{supplies} the pool with what it produces --- its plants and feedstock, where
production-side policies such as per-litre credits act --- and \textbf{draws} what it consumes,
where use-side policies such as mandates, eligibility and fuel taxes act. The market arbitrates;
each region's net flow is tracked, without routes or transport costs. That is the mechanism the
objective is about: a mandate in A pulls in the cheap feedstock, and B pays more for its fuel.
Its volume side exists in the kernel (\S\ref{sec:pool}); its plumbing through AeroMAPS has been
checked with an explicit stand-in for the market (\S\ref{sec:trade}). \textbf{Its price side is
D5.}

\subsection{Tuning policies}

Tuning is an \textbf{outer optimisation} over policy parameters with the market as the inner
model --- a GEMSEO scenario, not an MDA; an MPEC in the terms of \S\ref{sec:mcp}. It needs no
change to the market's foresight. Sensitivities exist on each smooth piece of the solution and
only as subgradients where the active set changes, so designs of experiments or
derivative-free methods are the safer first choice. With demand inside the kernel the outcomes
vary continuously with policy, which makes this better behaved. \textbf{Measured by D4.}

\subsection{MCP, MPEC, EPEC}
\label{sec:mcp}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=3pt]
  \item[Optimisation] --- what the kernel is. One objective, one solve; its optimality
    conditions \emph{are} the equilibrium, whenever that equilibrium is ``as if'' one planner
    minimised total cost.
  \item[MCP] --- mixed complementarity problem. Every agent's optimality conditions and the
    market-clearing conditions written directly, as pairs ``either this quantity is zero, or
    its margin is'': $x \ge 0$, $F(x) \ge 0$, $x\,F(x) = 0$. Every convex optimisation can be
    written so --- that is its KKT system --- but an MCP also covers equilibria no objective
    gives: price-dependent support, recycled revenue, agents with different discount rates or
    beliefs. Cost: existence and uniqueness are not guaranteed, and the solvers (PATH) need a
    reasonable start.
  \item[MPEC] --- one leader optimising against an equilibrium: the regulator chooses a
    mandate, the market responds. This is policy tuning.
  \item[EPEC] --- several leaders, each an MPEC, in equilibrium with one another: every region
    setting its own policy while anticipating the global market and the others'. Non-convex;
    may have no equilibrium or several. Research grade.
\end{description}

\noindent Neither MCP nor EPEC is needed now. Keeping the kernel's optimality conditions explicit
--- as the identity tests do --- keeps a later move to an MCP a reformulation, not a rewrite.

\section{A first step: one global pool in the kernel}
\label{sec:pool}

The volume side of the pool only: each region supplies it with what it produces and draws what
it consumes, and the flows are tracked. How pooling moves each region's \emph{price} is D5's
question and is deliberately not answered --- the prices are recorded, not interpreted.

\subsection{What the kernel now builds}

One new, optional input, a flag per pathway (\var{pooled}). Without it the kernel builds exactly
the programme it built before. With it, a region's two sides separate:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{4.4cm}p{7.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Side} & \textbf{Variables} & \textbf{Constraints that act there} \\
\midrule
produces & $q_{r,p,t}$, as before & cost, saturation, hard cap, growth limit --- plants and feedstock \\
consumes & $d_{r,p,t}$ for pooled pathways & energy balance, obligations, eligibility --- airlines and policy \\
pool & --- & $\sum_r q_{r,p,t} = \sum_r d_{r,p,t}$ per pooled pathway and year \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent A pathway not flagged stays local ($q = d$). New outputs: production, the net flow
(production minus consumption; it sums to zero over regions exactly) and the pool price (the
multiplier on the pool balance). Two identities hold and are tested: at every region
\emph{producing} a pooled fuel, pool price = cost + capacity rent + growth-limit rent, net of
next year's; wherever a region \emph{draws} it, its delivered marginal price for that fuel is
the pool price. Costs are read as the \emph{producing} region's; a cost levied where fuel is
burnt --- a carbon tax on use --- has no place yet.

\subsection{Flows are not unique, so a second solve picks the least trade}

Two regions making the same fuel at the same cost with capacity to spare can split the pool's
supply any way at the same total cost, and an interior-point solver returns the middle of that
set: each region producing for the other for no reason --- 35.1\,EJ traded over 2020--2050 where
the pool needed 1.7. So a pooled solve is followed by a second one that fixes the optimal cost
as a constraint and minimises the exported volume. The prices stay those of the first solve,
which is exact: in a convex programme every optimal primal point pairs with every optimal dual
point. It costs a few milliseconds. Pool prices of a fuel nobody draws are an interval, and are
pinned to its lower end --- the same remedy as the compliance price without an obligation.

\subsection{The bench}

Kernel only, at \textbf{fixed demand} taken from the converged hard-cap run of
\S\ref{sec:five}, so traffic does not respond to pooling. The separate case, re-solved, returns
that run's volumes to $3.3\times10^{-8}$ of demand; an independent year-by-year LP agrees with
the kernel's cost to $1.3\times10^{-7}$ in every year.

\subsection{What the pool determines, and what it does not}

The least-trade pass pins \textbf{how much} each region exports, not \textbf{which fuel}. Once an
exporter runs two fuels at their caps, both earn a rent and both sell at the price of the
marginal fuel above them, so shipping either costs the same. Measured directly --- the smallest
and largest net flow of each fuel over all points optimal in cost \emph{and} in traded volume:
with only HEFA capped (waste oil in A, 2025--2039) every flow is determined to the MJ; from 2040,
A exports 2.42\,EJ/yr and its HEFA share can be anything from 0 to 2.42.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pool_flows.png}
\caption{Region A's net exports by fuel. The dashed total is determined; the grey area marks the
years where its split between fuels is not.}
\label{fig:poolflows}
\end{figure}

\noindent This matters: which fuel a region burns decides the emission factor its CO$_2$ carries
and, at $w<1$, its average price; and an arbitrary split in a coupled loop is a quantity that
can flicker between sweeps, which is the failure of \S\ref{sec:fix}. Differing use-side carbon
taxes or eligibility rules would break these ties by themselves (read off the stationarity
conditions, not yet measured); a uniform carbon tax would not.

\textbf{Decided.} The market will say which fuel is traded, once it has the logic to --- use-side
costs among it. Until then an \textbf{explicit matrix} stands in for the market's flow logic,
and the first thing to establish is whether the multi-regional plumbing carries flows at all.
The carbon tax goes to the consumer side; how far that moves the market is for later.

\section{Plumbing for flows between regions: an explicit matrix}
\label{sec:trade}

\textbf{No market logic here.} The flows are stated by hand, as a sourcing matrix, and the only
question is whether the multi-regional process carries them: a region producing what it does
not burn, each side reaching the models that belong to it, every MJ accounted for once. The
kernel's pool is not used; it is the market logic meant to replace the matrix later.

\subsection{What was added}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.2cm}p{11.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Piece} & \textbf{What it does} \\
\midrule
\var{FuelTrade} (global model) & Reads every region's \var{\{p\}\_energy\_consumption}, applies the matrix, emits each region's \var{\{p\}\_energy\_production} and net export, and every tracked flow. Prospective years only. \\
\var{fuel\_trade} flag (under \var{regionalisation}) & Tells every region that production is no longer consumption; same route as \var{fuel\_market}. \\
Resources follow production & Feedstock use computed on \textbf{production}; CO$_2$ stays on \textbf{consumption}. Off by default. \\
Guards & Flag and model declared together or not at all; \var{unified\_mda} only; bottom-up pathways refused under trade. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The matrix, per pathway, gives each consuming region the shares it sources from each
producing region; each row sums to one, and anything not listed is made where it is burnt. On the
bench, all the HEFA region B burns is made in A, and a quarter of A's kerosene is made in B.

Nothing structural changed: each region is still a full, namespaced AeroMAPS process in one
\var{MDAChain}, and \var{FuelTrade} joins \var{FuelClearing} among the global models. The two new
couplings --- consumption to production, production to the aggregator --- are feed-forward, so
trade adds no loop: the MDA takes the same number of sweeps with and without it.

\subsection{The checks}

Four full two-region runs on the five-pathway bench --- standard mode and market mode, each
without and with trade. Worst relative error, the same in both modes:

\midrule

\noindent What moves, 2050: region A burns 9.8\,EJ of HEFA and makes 24.2; B burns 14.3 and makes

\subsection{What this does not do yet}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Nothing reads production except feedstock.} Costs are still each region's own:
    B pays B's cost for HEFA made in A --- identical on this bench, not in general. This is where
    trade first touches prices, deferred with the rest of the price interaction.
  \item \textbf{With the market on, caps would act on consumption}, since \var{FuelClearing} clears
    separate regional markets. A real combination needs the pool of \S\ref{sec:pool} to decide
    the flows --- which is its point.
  \item Bottom-up pathways are refused under trade, and the matrix is constant in time.
\end{itemize}

\section{Open items}
\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Join the pool to the plumbing.} The pool is in the kernel, the flows are carried
    through AeroMAPS, the discipline still clears separate markets. Before wiring: a use-side cost
    input, and a rule for which fuel is traded where the market leaves it open.
  \item \textbf{The step-2 diagnostics} D1--D5 (\S\ref{sec:diagnostics}), D1 first.
  \item \textbf{One obligation per region in the discipline}; the sub-mandate and regional
    eligibility are kernel-only, and $\lambda^{S}$ is computed but not published.
  \item \textbf{Three undiagnosed non-convergences}, all where every eligible route is at its
    limit: \var{all\_capped} at $w=0$, and capacities scaled $\times0.8$ and $\times0.65$ at $w=1$.
  \item \textbf{No test asserts that the demand adjustment vanishes at convergence} --- the
    guard on the price/volume consistency the discipline's reconciliation depends on.
  \item \textbf{\var{capacity\_limit} is one number per pathway}, for every region and year.
  \item \textbf{Two settings a user can set badly with no warning}: the demand slope and the MDA
    tolerance against the solver's.
  \item \textbf{The buy-out payment} does not enter the cost chain.
  \item \textbf{\var{cvxpy} as a proper optional dependency} before the mode ships.
\end{enumerate}
